Abstract
The Interpretive Convergence Theory of Law
Son, Jeyoun
This paper develops an alternative to the two dominant approaches in juris-
prudence ―legal positivism which emphasizes master rule or pedigree, and 
moral interpretivism― by offering a new account of how legal norms arise and 
why they bind. According to this view, the normativity of law emerges through 
a process of interpretive convergence within a community, and law itself is gen-
erated via the discursive transformation of authoritative acts (such as statutes or 
judicial decisions) and other legally oriented acts into legal meaning. Legal institu-
tions (legislatures, courts, agencies), along with various social actors, perform acts 
intended to influence behavior within a legal framework, but those acts acquire 
legal content and force only through their uptake and iterative interpretation by 
participants in a shared discourse. Roughly, the law is the product of convergent 
interpretive engagement with the outputs of legal acts and legally meaningful 
social practices, yielding an emergent set of norms that the community comes 
to treat as binding. I call this view the ‘Interpretive Convergence Theory of Law’ 
because it maintains that legal obligations are those normative propositions that 
crystallize from the collective interpretive practices of the legal community. In 
what follows, I motivate this theory by highlighting a foundational puzzle about 
legal normativity and meaning, critique the prevailing positivist and interpretiv-
ist accounts, and then articulate the positive emergentist view in detail. I argue 
that this approach illuminates how law’s content and its authority are grounded 
in social practice rather than in any single rule or moral criterion. The theory 
explains how legal norms achieve their binding force through mutual expecta-
